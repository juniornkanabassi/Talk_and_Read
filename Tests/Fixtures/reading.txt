The harbour town of Marlowe Quay woke slowly on 14 March 2027. By 6:45 a.m. the first ferry had left, carrying 212 passengers and a van full of fresh bread. "You'll miss it if you dawdle," shouted Mrs. Okafor, waving her umbrella at a boy who was, in fact, already running.

At the council office, the new NHS liaison, Dr. Priya Raman, spread out her notes. The agenda was short but dense:

1. Repair the sea wall before the autumn storms.
2. Approve a 3.5 million pound budget for the lifeboat station.
3. Replace forty-two broken streetlights along the old high-street.
4. Reply to the BBC about the documentary crew.

"None of this is glamorous," she admitted, "but a well-run town is a quiet one." The clerk smiled and noted that the UK average repair time for a sea wall was 18 months, whereas Marlowe's was closer to 27.

Outside, a retired engineer named Tomas Lindqvist was explaining the tide tables to anyone who would listen. His latest chart, published at https://example.org/marlowe/tides, showed high water at 11:20 and again just after midnight. Nobody questioned him, because he had been right about the flood of 1998, and about the three storms since.

By noon the market square was crowded. A baker sold out of rye loaves by 12:15; a fishmonger shouted prices in a rough, cheerful sing-song; and two children argued about whether a crab could be considered a pet. "It has a name," said the smaller one, firmly. "Kevin. He's a well-behaved crab."

Later, as the light turned gold over the water, the ferry returned. It was 4:05 p.m. exactly, and the passengers, tired, sunburnt and smelling faintly of diesel, stepped onto the quay. Some were going home. Others, like the nervous young teacher with two suitcases, were just arriving, and for them the whole town seemed to hold its breath.

"Welcome to Marlowe," said Mrs. Okafor, once again armed with her umbrella. "Mind the gap, mind the gulls, and mind your manners."
